# Snakebite

## Common symptoms
- Puncture marks or bite wound, often with pain
- Swelling and redness spreading from the bite site
- Nausea or vomiting
- Weakness or dizziness
- Difficulty breathing or swallowing (in severe envenomation)
- Bleeding from gums, nose, or the bite wound

## Ask the patient about
- When did the bite happen, and can the snake be described or identified (without risking another bite)?
- What part of the body was bitten, and how much swelling or pain is present?
- Any difficulty breathing, swallowing, drooping eyelids, or blurred vision?
- Any bleeding from the wound, gums, or nose?
- Was anything applied to the wound already (tourniquet, cutting, sucking) — these should generally be avoided?
- Any dizziness, weakness, or fainting since the bite?

## Red flags — seek urgent/emergency care immediately
- Difficulty breathing, swallowing, or drooping eyelids
- Rapidly spreading swelling or severe pain
- Bleeding from gums, nose, or the wound that won't stop
- Dizziness, fainting, or feeling faint
- Any snakebite at all should be treated as a potential emergency, since venom effects can be delayed

## Plain-language guidance
Keep the person calm and still, keep the bitten limb at or below heart level, remove tight clothing or jewelry near the bite, and get to a hospital as quickly as possible — do not cut, suck, apply ice, or tie a tight tourniquet on the wound. Every snakebite should be evaluated by a doctor promptly, even if symptoms seem mild at first, since some venom effects appear later.
